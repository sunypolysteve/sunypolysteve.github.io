#173 2026-09-28T16:22:16.685681Z Brookhaven National Laboratory Data Conference
what is this talk about.

In plain terms, Simoncelli is using AI to design better materials, like the ones in solar cells, by predicting how their flaws behave.

The problem runs like this:
1. Textbook materials are perfect crystals with repeating atoms, and physics can calculate those easily.
2. Real materials are full of defects: misplaced atoms and boundaries where structures meet. Those defects limit performance. In perovskite solar cells, for example, they make the material break down.
3. Accurately simulating defects requires quantum physics across thousands of atoms, which is too expensive to compute. That's the Kohn problem.

His proposed solution is physics-aware AI. The models are trained on accurate quantum calculations and constrained to obey physical laws, so they can predict behavior at realistic scales quickly without breaking the physics.

The goal is to go from atoms to technological applications: use these predictions to engineer materials that avoid or tolerate defects, and so get more stable, efficient solar cells.

This connects to the day's theme, since here "trust" in AI comes from building physics into the model rather than from human gates.

#174 2026-09-28T16:22:49.155234Z Brookhaven National Laboratory Data Conference
explain what that means, physics into the model?

A standard AI model learns only from examples. Show it enough data and it finds patterns, but it has no idea why things happen. It can produce answers that look right yet violate basic physics, like predicting energy appearing from nowhere or a material behaving differently just because you rotated it. That's the plausibility problem again.

"Building physics in" means constraining the model so certain wrong answers are impossible. A few common ways:

  • Symmetry: Physics doesn't change if you rotate or shift a crystal, so the model's architecture is designed so its predictions can't change either. It doesn't have to learn this; it's hardwired.
  • Conservation laws: Energy and mass must balance, so the model is structured or penalized to respect that.
  • Training on physics, not just observations: The model learns from accurate quantum calculations, so it inherits real physical relationships.
  • Predicting fundamental quantities: Instead of guessing a final property directly, the model predicts something basic, like the energy between atoms, and standard physics equations derive the rest.

An analogy: a regular model is a student who memorized thousands of worked problems, while a physics-aware model is a student who also knows the rules and can't write down an answer that breaks them.

The payoff is that it needs less data, generalizes to new materials better, and is more trustworthy outside the cases it was trained on.
